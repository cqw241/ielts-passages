## 1. Main Reading Article

### The Numbers Are Equal. The Chances Are Not.

**A.** Imagine two students marked identically in a national school database: both are enrolled. One can walk to class and study in the evening; the other spends hours travelling and caring for younger relatives. On paper, their opportunities appear equal. In reality, the distance between registering for school and finishing it may be enormous. That is the central tension in UNESCO's 2026 Gender Report, released around the International Day of the Girl Child. By 2024, global enrolment had reached gender parity from pre-primary through upper secondary education. This is a milestone, but the headline figure can mask differences that shape who actually graduates.

**B.** Long-term progress is real, not an illusion. In Cambodia, girls were 22 percentage points behind boys in primary-school completion in 1981; by 2024, they were 11 points ahead. Nepal also eliminated a 33-point gap in lower-secondary completion between 1990 and 2024. These changes show that apparently entrenched disparities can shift dramatically over a generation. Yet efforts to close the gap have been uneven, and the direction of inequality is not always the same. Some countries now have higher completion among girls, while others still struggle to bring girls into and through secondary school. Celebrating progress and questioning its distribution are compatible positions.

**C.** One reason the statistics need care is that an enrolment figure is not the same as a completion rate. A child may appear on a school register but leave before gaining a qualification. Moreover, boys face obstacles too. UNESCO estimates that, of the 273 million children and young people out of school worldwide in 2024, about 140 million were boys and 133 million were girls. Boys are also more likely to enter school late or repeat grades in some settings. These facts complicate any claim that educational disadvantage always runs in one direction. The question is not which gender deserves attention, but which students are being left behind, and why.

**D.** A second problem lies inside national averages. In Angola, girls and boys from the wealthiest households have reached parity in upper-secondary completion; among the poorest households, only 30 girls finish for every 100 boys. In India, the equivalent ratio is 59 girls for every 100 boys in rural areas, despite parity in urban areas. The disparity is not simply between countries but within them. Poverty, distance from school and care responsibilities can overlap, and each difficulty may compound the others. A national average may therefore conceal the very groups that require the most support. Gender cannot be understood in isolation from income and geography.

**E.** Child marriage makes the problem more urgent. According to UNESCO, the global share of girls married before their eighteenth birthday fell from 25% in 2000 to 18% in 2025, a substantial improvement. Nevertheless, the rate remained about 31% in sub-Saharan Africa according to the report. Marriage, pregnancy and interrupted education can form a feedback loop: leaving school may increase vulnerability to early marriage, while marriage and parenthood can make continuing school much harder. UNESCO estimates that six million pregnant girls and young mothers aged 10–19 were out of school in sub-Saharan Africa in 2024. Fewer than 5% of pregnant and parenting girls in the region return to school.

**F.** Legal protection matters, but a right written into law does not automatically create a place in a classroom. UNESCO reports that 58% of countries legally guarantee education without discrimination on the basis of sex or gender, while only 35% explicitly protect the educational rights of pregnant and parenting girls. A young mother may be entitled to re-enter school yet still have no affordable childcare, safe transport or suitable sanitation facilities. She may also encounter stigma from classmates or staff. Without practical support, students can fall through the cracks even when a formal policy appears inclusive. The real test is whether a stated right can be exercised.

**G.** The same distinction applies to money. Removing tuition fees helps, but families may still face indirect costs, including transport costs and uniforms, as well as lost income when a teenager stays in school instead of working. Economists call the value of the next-best alternative the opportunity cost; for poor households, such opportunity costs can be decisive. Financial constraints may combine with fears about safety or strong expectations that daughters should manage household duties. It would be misleading to treat every early departure as a simple lack of ambition. A family may place a high value on education but lack realistic alternatives. Grants, school meals and reliable transport can change what is practically possible.

**H.** No single measure is likely to solve such a layered problem. UNESCO points to evidence from 14 African countries suggesting that making lower-secondary education both free and compulsory increased girls' educational attainment by 1.6 years compared with making it free alone. The report also describes girl-friendly schools in Burkina Faso that raised enrolment by 19 percentage points overall, with larger gains for girls. Such findings support a policy package rather than a single slogan: affordable schooling, safer journeys, protection against violence and assistance with childcare. They do not mean every programme will work equally well everywhere; design and local conditions still matter.

**I.** This creates a challenge for policy implementation. Governments need data detailed enough to track progress by income, location, gender and age, not merely a single national total. They also need to investigate structural barriers rather than assume the reasons for leaving school are obvious. In one district, a distant secondary school might be the main obstacle; elsewhere, childcare or harassment might matter more. Support should be tailored to local needs and tested against actual completion, not only initial enrolment. Flexible timetables and second-chance pathways may help students whose education has already been interrupted, provided the qualifications they offer are recognized and useful.

**J.** There is a deeper lesson in how equality is measured. If schools are judged only by the number of students who enter, they may receive credit for opportunities that some learners cannot use. If success is defined only by girls catching up with boys, disadvantaged boys may disappear from view. Better measures ask who completes school, under what conditions, and with what choices afterwards. Efforts to translate formal access into lasting opportunity require more than a balanced register: they demand attention to the obstacles that decide whether students can stay. The point of gender parity is not to produce equal numbers on paper, but to make a meaningful education possible.

### A. Suggested Active Vocabulary — 6 Items

These expressions are especially valuable for independent speaking, essay writing and professional communication.

#### 1. ★ mask (v.)

- **Definition:** To hide or prevent something from being clearly noticed.
- **In this article:** National statistics can hide important differences between groups.
- **From the text (A):** "the headline figure can mask differences that shape who actually graduates."
- **Useful patterns:** mask a problem; mask underlying inequalities; mask the true extent of something.
- **Another context:** Strong sales figures may mask a decline in customer loyalty.

**Usage insight:** *Mask* often suggests that an apparently positive or neutral fact makes a less favourable reality difficult to see. It does not necessarily imply deliberate deception.

#### 2. ★ close the gap (verb phrase)

- **Definition:** To reduce or eliminate a difference between two groups, situations or levels of achievement.
- **In this article:** Reducing differences in educational completion between girls and boys.
- **From the text (B):** "Yet efforts to close the gap have been uneven"
- **Useful patterns:** close the gap between A and B; close the skills gap; help close the gap.
- **Another context:** The company introduced a mentoring programme to close the gap in technical skills between experienced and newly recruited employees.

**Usage insight:** *Narrow the gap* emphasises reduction; *close the gap* often suggests the goal of eliminating the difference entirely.

#### 3. ★ compound (v.)

- **Definition:** To make an existing problem or difficulty worse by adding another problem.
- **In this article:** Financial problems, geographical distance and family duties can intensify one another.
- **From the text (D):** "each difficulty may compound the others."
- **Useful patterns:** compound the problem; compound existing difficulties; compound the effects of something.
- **Another context:** Delayed payments compounded the company's cash-flow problems.

**Usage insight:** *Compound* is particularly useful when several negative factors reinforce each other. It is stronger and more precise than simply saying *make worse*.

#### 4. ★ be entitled to (verb phrase)

- **Definition:** To have a legal, contractual or established right to receive or do something.
- **In this article:** Pregnant and parenting students may have a recognised right to continue their education.
- **From the text (F):** "A young mother may be entitled to re-enter school"
- **Useful patterns:** be entitled to a refund; be entitled to paid leave; be entitled to appeal a decision.
- **Another context:** Under the revised contract, employees are entitled to additional paid leave.

**Usage insight:** Use **be entitled to + noun** or **be entitled to + infinitive**. Having a right to something is different from being practically able to exercise that right.

#### 5. ★ fall through the cracks (idiom)

- **Definition:** To fail to receive necessary attention, assistance or protection because a system does not adequately recognise one's situation.
- **In this article:** Students can be overlooked even when education policies appear inclusive.
- **From the text (F):** "students can fall through the cracks even when a formal policy appears inclusive."
- **Useful patterns:** fall through the cracks in the system; prevent vulnerable people from falling through the cracks.
- **Another context:** Freelancers sometimes fall through the cracks of traditional employee-benefit schemes.

**Usage insight:** This expression is especially useful when discussing complicated organisations, administrative systems and people whose needs are not clearly assigned to anyone.

#### 6. ★ be tailored to (verb phrase)

- **Definition:** To be specially designed or adjusted to suit particular needs, conditions or people.
- **In this article:** Educational support should reflect the actual difficulties faced in different communities.
- **From the text (I):** "Support should be tailored to local needs"
- **Useful patterns:** be tailored to individual needs; tailor a programme to an audience; a tailored approach.
- **Another context:** The onboarding programme is tailored to employees with different levels of experience.

**Usage insight:** Both active and passive constructions are useful:

- The trainer **tailored the course to** the participants' needs.
- The course **was tailored to** the participants' needs.

## 6. Deep Sentence Analysis

### Sentence 1 — Describing Multiple Causes

**Original (D):**

> Poverty, distance from school and care responsibilities can overlap, and each difficulty may compound the others.

**Simpler paraphrase:**

Financial problems, long journeys to school and family duties may happen together and make one another worse.

**Structure and grammar:**

The sentence combines two independent clauses using **and**.

- Clause 1: *Poverty, distance from school and care responsibilities can overlap.*
- Clause 2: *Each difficulty may compound the others.*

The first clause establishes coexistence. The second establishes interaction.

*Can* and *may* indicate possibility rather than certainty. The word *each* treats the difficulties individually, while *the others* refers to the remaining difficulties.

This structure is particularly useful for explaining complex problems with interacting causes.

**Transfer example:**

> Staff shortages, unrealistic deadlines and poor planning can overlap, and each problem may compound the others.

### Sentence 2 — Contrasting a Right With a Practical Limitation

**Original (F):**

> A young mother may be entitled to re-enter school yet still have no affordable childcare, safe transport or suitable sanitation facilities.

**Simpler paraphrase:**

A young mother may have the legal right to return to school but lack the support necessary to attend.

**Structure and grammar:**

The sentence uses the contrastive pattern:

**may + verb phrase + yet still + verb phrase**

The word *yet* introduces a surprising or important contrast, while *still* emphasises that the difficulty remains.

Notice that both parts share the same subject and modal verb:

- A young mother **may be entitled to** re-enter school.
- A young mother **may still have** no affordable childcare.

The compact structure avoids repeating unnecessary words.

**Transfer example:**

> An employee may be entitled to flexible working hours yet still hesitate to request them because of workplace expectations.

### Sentence 3 — Challenging the Way Success Is Defined

**Original (J):**

> If success is defined only by girls catching up with boys, disadvantaged boys may disappear from view.

**Simpler paraphrase:**

If we judge success only by whether girls achieve the same results as boys, we may overlook boys who are also struggling.

**Structure and grammar:**

This is a conditional sentence using a passive construction.

- **Condition:** *If success is defined only by girls catching up with boys*
- **Consequence:** *disadvantaged boys may disappear from view*

The passive *is defined* focuses attention on the method of measurement rather than on who defines it.

The expression **by + -ing** identifies the criterion used to judge success.

*Disappear from view* is metaphorical: it means becoming overlooked or no longer receiving attention.

**Transfer example:**

> If business success is defined only by revenue growth, employee well-being may disappear from view.

**Writing insight:** This structure is useful for challenging a limited definition, measurement or assumption in analytical essays.

## 7. IELTS Extension

### Writing Task 2

**Question:**

Some people believe that making secondary education free is sufficient to provide equal educational opportunities. Others argue that governments must also address wider social and economic barriers.

Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Suggested argument outline**

**Introduction**
- Acknowledge the importance of removing financial barriers.
- Argue that free schooling is necessary in many contexts but not sufficient on its own.

**Body Paragraph 1 — Why free education matters**
- Tuition fees may prevent low-income families from keeping children in school.
- Publicly funded schooling can reduce direct educational costs.
- Financial support can help close the gap between disadvantaged and wealthier students.

**Body Paragraph 2 — Why broader support is necessary**
- National averages may mask structural barriers.
- Transport, childcare, safety and social stigma may compound financial difficulties.
- Support should be tailored to the needs of particular communities.

**Conclusion**
- Equal access requires both affordable schooling and practical support.
- Policy success should be assessed through completion and meaningful opportunities, not enrolment alone.

## 8. Today You Learned

### Three Important Ideas

**1. Equal enrolment does not guarantee equal educational opportunity.**

Global gender parity is a major achievement, but it can coexist with substantial differences in completion rates across income groups and locations.

**2. Educational disadvantage often involves interacting causes.**

Poverty, early marriage, care responsibilities, distance and social stigma may reinforce one another. Understanding these interactions helps explain why a single solution may be insufficient.

**3. Effective policy requires more than formal rights.**

Legal protections matter, but governments must also ensure that students can exercise those rights through affordable, safe and practical arrangements.

### Six Expressions to Use Actively

1. **mask** — hide an important problem or difference.
2. **close the gap** — reduce inequality or differences in achievement.
3. **compound** — intensify an existing difficulty.
4. **be entitled to** — possess an established right.
5. **fall through the cracks** — be overlooked by a system.
6. **be tailored to** — be designed for particular needs.

### Three Reusable Collocations

- **mask underlying disparities**
- **compound existing difficulties**
- **be tailored to individual needs**
