## The Work That Continues After a Disease Milestone

### A

A public-health milestone can look like an ending even when it marks the beginning of a different responsibility. WHO's September announcement recognised Bhutan's achievement after the country maintained zero human deaths from dog-mediated rabies from June 2023 onwards. The validation concerns a defined public-health outcome, rather than the disappearance of every possible source of infection. Elimination within a country is not the same as worldwide eradication. This distinction matters because an imprecise celebration could encourage an equally imprecise response. The question is no longer simply how to reach a target, but how to preserve the conditions that made reaching it possible.

### B

Rabies illustrates the gap between possessing scientific tools and delivering their benefits. The disease is almost always fatal once clinical symptoms appear, yet deaths are preventable through timely action. After a possible exposure, prompt medical assessment and appropriate post-exposure care are essential; waiting for symptoms is dangerous. Such care includes wound washing, vaccination and additional medicines when indicated. An effective intervention must reach the person who needs it, not merely exist in a national plan. For public services, this suggests a demanding standard: success depends both on the availability of a treatment and on the sequence of decisions that allows someone to receive it.

### C

Prevention also takes place outside the clinic. WOAH identifies vaccination of at least 70% of dogs in at-risk areas as a central measure for reducing human cases to zero. Coverage describes the share of the target population reached, whereas a threshold identifies a level relevant to an intended effect. Susceptible animals remain important because infection can circulate where protection is inadequate. Dog vaccination and appropriate human care are complementary measures, not substitutes for one another. A national average should therefore prompt questions about local gaps. An impressive overall percentage could conceal communities that remain difficult to reach; this is an analytical possibility, not a finding about Bhutan.

### D

The One Health approach treats human, animal and environmental health as connected fields requiring joint action. Coordination turns that principle into shared responsibilities and workable communication. A fragmented system may leave each department performing its own task while nobody follows the whole chain. WHO's account stresses the importance of information sharing and cooperation across sectors. Consider a hypothetical referral from a village service to a better-equipped clinic: it is useful only if the destination can respond and the patient can get there. Only when responsibilities are clear can information reliably lead to action. A joint committee is a starting point, not proof that those practical connections work.

### E

Surveillance provides the continuing observation needed to identify changes in disease patterns. However, underreporting can make a weak detection system appear reassuringly quiet. WHO notes that documented rabies counts can differ from estimates because cases go unreported. The implication is that verification should examine how evidence was produced, as well as what the final number says. Imagine two districts reporting no cases: one investigates suspected infections consistently, while the other receives few reports from distant villages. Their identical totals would not justify identical confidence. This hypothetical comparison does not imply deliberate concealment. It shows why the capacity to detect a problem belongs inside any assessment of its apparent absence.

### F

Equitable access requires attention to the obstacles facing different communities. A useful development over roughly the past two years was Gavi's June 2024 announcement of support for human rabies vaccines used after exposure. The programme covered vaccine procurement and associated supplies, but excluded dog vaccines and rabies immunoglobulins. Funding one component therefore does not automatically finance an entire response. A logistical bottleneck can prevent supplies from reaching a remote clinic even when national stocks are sufficient. That example is hypothetical, but its policy implication is clear: planners should examine the journey from purchasing a product to making it accessible. Equal quantities delivered to unequal settings need not produce equally usable services.

### G

The allocation of limited resources creates a familiar trade-off between immediate demands and future protection. A cost-effective measure delivers worthwhile results relative to its cost, rather than simply having the lowest price. WOAH describes mass dog vaccination as a key means of tackling rabies at its animal source. Nevertheless, the economic case for a particular programme still depends on local costs, needs and implementation. An incentive to reward visible activity may favour a large launch over routine follow-up. In this article's judgement, budgets should give greater recognition to maintaining useful services. This does not establish that prevention deserves every available resource or that all proposed programmes represent good value.

### H

Continuity of protection depends on institutions remaining attentive after public attention has moved elsewhere. Complacency can develop when a previous danger no longer feels immediate. WHO says Bhutan will maintain free access to post-exposure prophylaxis across its districts, continue dog vaccination and strengthen coordination across the border with neighbouring Indian states. These are commitments to preserve the achievement, not evidence that all future risk has vanished. Reintroduction remains a possibility against which functioning systems must be prepared. Although success may reduce the visibility of a threat, it does not remove the need for readiness. Continued effort is part of the achievement itself, rather than an admission that the achievement was meaningless.

### I

For other countries, the challenge is to learn without assuming that one administrative model will work everywhere. Accountability requires clarity about who must explain a decision and correct a failure. A government seeking to replicate an outcome may need substantial adaptation of the means used to achieve it. Population distribution, existing services and public trust could all affect the choices available. What deserves to travel is a set of tested principles, not necessarily an identical organisational chart. Clear goals, connected services and credible evidence can guide local decisions without prescribing every detail. This is an argument for careful learning rather than either mechanical copying or the rejection of outside experience.

### J

Bhutan's milestone offers a constructive way to think about progress: celebrate a demonstrated result while asking what will keep it secure. Measured only by the absence of reported deaths, a programme could appear successful for the wrong reason. Assessed alongside reliable detection and accessible services, the same result becomes more informative. Wider progress will remain contingent on choices about delivery, funding and cooperation. It is possible that stronger partnerships will help more countries reach similar goals, but that is a conditional prospect, not an assured forecast. The most convincing public-health success leaves behind institutions capable of noticing trouble and responding before a preventable problem becomes another headline.

**Evidence lens:** The dated announcements and attributed public-health explanations are sourced facts. Districts, referrals and supply-chain examples are explicitly hypothetical. Claims about what governments should prioritise are the author's policy judgements. “May,” “could” and “contingent on” mark uncertainty. No claim is made that rabies has been eradicated worldwide or that a vaccination target guarantees protection in every setting.

### REVIEW ANSWER KEY

1. **resilience** — normally uncountable: “build resilience,” not “build a resilience.”
2. **underlying** — an adjective identifying the deeper cause.
3. **conceal** — “A national total may conceal regional inequalities.” It need not imply deliberate deception.
4. **labour force participation** — includes employed and officially unemployed people; it is not the employment rate alone.
5. **underemployment** — the person has work but lacks desired, available working hours.
6. **purchasing power** — possible answer: “Rising prices reduce the purchasing power of an unchanged salary.” Your sentence should relate income to what it buys.
7. **shift** — the complete target item is **demographic shift**.
8. **productivity** — producing the same output with less labour time raises labour productivity.
9. **trade-off** — use “a trade-off between A and B.”
10. **job security** — continued employment, rather than workplace safety.
11. **preliminary** — early findings remain useful but may be updated.
12. **revision** — “a revision to” is the appropriate noun–preposition pattern.
13. **aggregate** — the adjective describes a combined measure.
14. **account for** — replace “of” with “for”; here the phrase means constitute a share.
15. **composition** — “We should examine the composition of the workforce, not only its size.”
16. **discouraged workers** — a defined subgroup outside the official unemployment count, not all unhappy jobseekers.
17. **nominal** — the contrast is with inflation-adjusted or real earnings.
18. **erode** — gradual weakening; it takes “public trust” as its direct object.
19. **disproportionate** — the comparison is to household resources, not necessarily to the fee charged to others.
20. **contingent on** — “Funding is contingent on approval.” “Upon” is also grammatical.

### 01. milestone — ★ CORE VOCABULARY

**IPA / part of speech:** /ˈmaɪlstəʊn/ — countable noun.  
**Definition:** An important stage in a longer process.  
**In context:** Bhutan's recognised achievement within continuing disease-control work.  
**Article:** “A public-health milestone can look like an ending even when it marks the beginning of a different responsibility.”  
**Collocation:** reach a milestone.

## FIVE SENTENCES FOR DEEP ANALYSIS

### Sentence 1 — a precise comparison

**Original, C:** “Coverage describes the share of the target population reached, whereas a threshold identifies a level relevant to an intended effect.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** Coverage tells us how many of the intended recipients were reached. A threshold tells us which level matters for a particular result.

**Structure:** A finite main clause followed by a contrasting finite clause introduced by “whereas.”

**Main clause:** “Coverage describes the share of the target population reached.”

**Dependent clauses:** “whereas a threshold identifies a level relevant to an intended effect” expresses contrast. “Reached” can be expanded to the reduced passive relative “that has been reached,” modifying “target population.”

**Important grammar:** Parallel present-simple verbs, “describes” and “identifies,” make the comparison easy to follow. “Relevant to an intended effect” is an adjective phrase, not a finite clause.

**Important vocabulary:** coverage; threshold; target population.

**Why it sounds academic:** The sentence separates two related concepts by identifying what each measures.

**New IELTS-style example:** Enrolment describes how many students join a course, whereas completion identifies how many finish it.

### Sentence 2 — inversion after a limiting expression

**Original, D:** “Only when responsibilities are clear can information reliably lead to action.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** Information can reliably produce action only if people know their responsibilities.

**Structure:** Fronted “only when” clause + inverted modal and subject + main verb phrase.

**Main clause:** “can information reliably lead to action.” In ordinary order: “information can reliably lead to action.”

**Dependent clause:** “when responsibilities are clear,” modified by “only,” sets a necessary condition for the claimed reliability.

**Important grammar:** The main clause inverts to “can information,” not “information can,” after the fronted limiting expression. The dependent clause keeps ordinary order: “responsibilities are.”

**Important vocabulary:** responsibilities; reliably; lead to action.

**Why it sounds academic:** It foregrounds a condition while keeping the causal claim explicit. Use this emphasis sparingly.

**New IELTS-style example:** Only when public transport is dependable can commuters realistically leave their cars at home.

### Sentence 3 — checking the production of evidence

**Original, E:** “The implication is that verification should examine how evidence was produced, as well as what the final number says.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** When checking a claim, we should inspect both the way its evidence was collected and the reported result.

**Structure:** An abstract noun subject + linking verb + a content clause containing two coordinated subordinate clauses.

**Main clause:** “The implication is …”

**Dependent clauses:** “that verification should examine …” supplies the implication's content. “How evidence was produced” and “what the final number says” function as things to be examined. The first is an embedded interrogative clause; the second can be understood as the content expressed by the number.

**Important grammar:** Embedded questions retain statement order: “how evidence was produced,” not “how was evidence produced.” The passive emphasises evidence production rather than a named researcher.

**Important vocabulary:** implication; verification; as well as.

**Why it sounds academic:** It links a conclusion to a concrete standard for assessing evidence, rather than merely asking for more data.

**New IELTS-style example:** The lesson is that universities should examine how students were assessed, as well as what their final grades show.

### Sentence 4 — a relative clause with a fronted preposition

**Original, H:** “Reintroduction remains a possibility against which functioning systems must be prepared.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** The disease could return, so functioning systems must be ready for that possibility.

**Structure:** Subject + linking verb + noun phrase modified by a defining relative clause.

**Main clause:** “Reintroduction remains a possibility.”

**Dependent clause:** “against which functioning systems must be prepared” modifies “possibility.” “Which” refers back to that possibility.

**Important grammar:** The preposition appears before the relative pronoun: “against which.” “Must be prepared” expresses a requirement for readiness. A less formal alternative is “a possibility that systems must prepare for.”

**Important vocabulary:** reintroduction; functioning systems; prepared against a risk.

**Why it sounds academic:** It connects an abstract risk directly to the response it requires, using a compact relative clause.

**New IELTS-style example:** Flooding remains a threat against which coastal communities must be protected.

### Sentence 5 — a reduced passive clause and an evaluation limit

**Original, J:** “Measured only by the absence of reported deaths, a programme could appear successful for the wrong reason.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** If we judge a programme only by whether deaths were reported, we might wrongly think it has succeeded.

**Structure:** Initial non-finite past-participle clause + main clause with “could.”

**Main clause:** “a programme could appear successful for the wrong reason.”

**Dependent clause:** “Measured only by the absence of reported deaths” is a reduced passive clause: “If it is measured only by …” Its understood subject is “a programme.”

**Important grammar:** Keep the subject of the reduced clause aligned with the main subject. “Measured only by exam results, teachers may overlook wellbeing” would create an unintended meaning: it is the teachers who are being measured.

**Important vocabulary:** measured by; absence; reported.

**Why it sounds academic:** The sentence identifies how a narrow assessment method can distort an inference, while “could” avoids claiming that distortion always occurs.

**New IELTS-style example:** Judged solely by ticket sales, a museum could appear successful despite failing to reach local residents.

## IELTS WRITING TASK 2

**Spend about 40 minutes on this task. Write at least 250 words.**

Governments should spend more money on preventing health problems than on treating people who are already ill.

To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your knowledge or experience.

### WRITING TASK 2 PLANNING

**Question type:** Opinion / extent of agreement. This differs from yesterday's discussion of two views.

**What the question is really asking:** Judge a relative spending priority. “More money … than” is stronger than “prevention is useful.” State whether you support that comparison universally, partly, or only under certain conditions.

**Possible thesis:** I support greater investment in effective prevention, but disagree with a universal rule that it must receive more funding than treatment, because an appropriate balance depends on local needs and existing services.

**Main argument 1:** Prevention can avoid harm and reduce future demand for treatment. Explain a plausible mechanism: vaccination reduces some infection risks; safer roads reduce injuries. Do not assume all preventive measures work equally well.

**Main argument 2:** People who are already ill still require accessible treatment. Cutting essential care to meet a fixed spending ratio could impose serious costs, especially where services are already limited.

**Counterargument:** Treatment can absorb resources that might prevent future illness. Acknowledge this opportunity cost, then argue for evidence-based allocation rather than an automatic ratio.

**Relevant examples:** Use an illustrative district choosing between outreach vaccination and maintaining urgent care. Explain why the two can be complementary. Label invented scenarios as examples; no fabricated statistics or named research are needed.

**Suggested paragraph structure:**

1. Introduce the issue and state the extent of your agreement.
2. Explain the value of increasing effective prevention, with a mechanism and example.
3. Explain why a universal spending rule is too rigid, recognising treatment needs and local variation.
4. Restate the position: stronger prevention alongside justified protection of essential treatment.

**Useful vocabulary:** intervention, coverage, equitable, allocation, cost-effective, complementary, accountability, bottleneck.

**Useful collocations:** equitable access to care; a cost-effective intervention; resource allocation; strengthen disease surveillance; remove logistical bottlenecks.

**Common mistakes to avoid:** Treating prevention and treatment as mutually exclusive; claiming prevention is always cheaper; supporting prevention without addressing the proposed spending comparison; discussing only rabies; making unsupported universal claims; giving a position in the conclusion that differs from the introduction.

### 5 BAND 7.5–8.5 LANGUAGE PATTERNS

These patterns can support sophisticated writing when used accurately and naturally; they do not guarantee a score.

1. **While there is a strong case for [policy], this does not justify [overly broad conclusion].** Concede a benefit while limiting the inference.
2. **Only when [necessary condition] can [subject] [achieve outcome].** Use modal–subject inversion after the fronted limiting clause.
3. **The question is not merely whether [action] is desirable, but how [resources or responsibilities] should be distributed.** Move from a general aim to a decision that needs justification.
4. **Assessed in terms of [criterion], [policy] may appear [judgement]; however, [additional consideration].** Keep the understood subject of “assessed” aligned with the main subject.
5. **What matters most is whether [proposal] addresses [need] without undermining [essential function].** Use a what-clause to emphasise an evaluation standard.
