## Reading Is a Skill We Must Keep Using

### A

Imagine an adult who can read a short message but struggles to compare two rental agreements. Calling this person either literate or illiterate would tell us little about the task they face. Literacy matters not only because words carry information, but because people must act on what those words mean. Foundational skills support everyday decisions that can otherwise depend on someone else's interpretation. Autonomy grows when a person can understand an important document without surrendering the decision to another reader. UNESCO's September literacy celebration provides a timely starting point for this discussion. Its wider significance lies in the relationship between learning and the choices that learning makes possible. UNESCO

### B

Proficiency is better understood as a continuum than as a switch that is either on or off. Educational attainment records completed levels of education, whereas reading ability concerns what someone can actually do with a text. The OECD's adult-skills findings, released in December 2024, reported declining or stagnant average literacy performance in most participating countries over the preceding decade. This is historical evidence, not a new September 2026 survey. OECD findings A disparity between qualifications and current abilities is therefore worth investigating. Stagnation in an average, however, does not establish that every individual has stopped learning. Nor can a national comparison alone identify the cause of one person's difficulty.

### C

The digital divide adds another layer to the problem. ITU estimated that 2.2 billion people remained offline in 2025, while stressing differences in connection quality and cost among those with access. ITU 2025 release Affordability matters because a service cannot be used consistently if its price competes with essential household spending. Yet a connection does not itself teach someone to navigate a complicated application form. Picture a hypothetical applicant who owns a phone but must search several pages to locate an exception to an eligibility rule. Access enables the attempt; understanding determines whether the person can complete it accurately. Better design could reduce that burden as well as better teaching.

### D

Once information is available, readers still need to discern what deserves their trust. A credible claim has support that can be examined, rather than merely a confident presentation. Misleading wording can remain technically accurate while directing attention away from an important qualification. An inference connects what a text states with a conclusion that the reader draws from it. For example, an advertisement saying that some participants improved does not show that most did, or that the advertised product caused the improvement. This invented example highlights a practical reading task: identify the size of the claim before accepting it. Teaching that habit would connect literacy lessons to decisions beyond the classroom.

### E

A useful lesson can scaffold a difficult task by supplying temporary support that is gradually withdrawn. A tutor might first demonstrate how to identify the main claim in a paragraph, then ask learners to practise with a different text. Retention requires more than a successful answer while the example remains visible. Later practice could reveal whether the learner can explain the idea again without the original prompt. Transferable skills become apparent when a learner uses an approach successfully in a new setting. These are proposed teaching choices, not results from a programme evaluated here. The aim is neither to leave a beginner unsupported nor to make the learner permanently dependent on a tutor.

### F

Adults may also face barriers that no worksheet can solve. Stigma can make asking for help feel like admitting a personal failure. A hypothetical parent working variable shifts might want to study but find that the same evening is not free each week. Providers could accommodate such constraints through flexible timetables, without assuming that flexibility removes the need for structure. Relevance matters when limited study time must compete with other responsibilities. Practising with a realistic school notice might therefore feel more purposeful than decoding unrelated sentences. These possibilities should be checked with learners themselves; an apparently convenient arrangement may still be difficult to use in everyday life.

### G

The person supporting the learner needs opportunities to develop too. A facilitator helps others participate and make progress rather than simply presenting material. In a 2026 account, UNESCO's Institute for Lifelong Learning described an online digital-skills course that had reached around 10,000 literacy educators since its December 2025 launch. Its format included eleven self-paced sessions. UIL course account Self-paced study allows participants to move through material according to their own timetable. Engagement, however, involves meaningful attention and participation, not merely opening a page. The reported reach is evidence of participation in an initiative, not by itself proof of improved reading outcomes among every learner taught by those educators.

### H

The benefits of literacy also extend beyond completing forms or obtaining work. Agency involves being able to pursue a goal and influence the circumstances affecting one's life. Civic participation can include reading a local proposal, discussing its implications and submitting an informed response. UNESCO presents literacy as a right connected to wider freedoms and participation. UNESCO literacy overview Consider a hypothetical community deciding how to use a public building: understandable documents make it easier for residents to question the available options. Reading does not guarantee agreement, but disagreement based on a shared understanding can be more useful than silence caused by confusion. Confidence and critical judgement need not pull in opposite directions.

### I

Governments nevertheless face choices about where to direct limited resources. The opportunity cost of one programme is the value of the best alternative use of the same resources. Adult-learning provision might compete with other educational needs, while also supporting parents, workers and community organisations. In this article's judgement, that wider role deserves serious consideration, but it does not justify every proposed course. Decision-makers could compare cost, accessibility and progress on meaningful tasks. They should also ask who is absent from the programme and why. A service that works well for those already able to attend may require changes before it can help people facing greater obstacles.

### J

The central challenge is to connect learning with repeated opportunities to use it. Libraries, workplaces and public services could reinforce skills by offering understandable material and occasions for purposeful reading. A course should leave learners better able to tackle unfamiliar tasks, rather than merely better at repeating its exercises. Whether new initiatives achieve that depends on teaching, access and the conditions in which people practise. International Literacy Day can draw attention to these needs, but attention must lead to decisions that learners can feel in their daily lives. Reading becomes a lasting resource when it helps someone ask a better question, make a considered choice and continue learning independently.

## FIVE SENTENCES FOR DEEP ANALYSIS

### Sentence 1 — a hypothetical consequence

**Original, A:** “Calling this person either literate or illiterate would tell us little about the task they face.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** Putting the person into one of two categories would not explain much about their particular difficulty.

**Structure:** An -ing clause as subject + modal verb phrase + a noun phrase containing a relative clause.

**Main clause:** “Calling this person either literate or illiterate would tell us little about the task they face.” The subject is the full clause beginning “Calling.”

**Dependent clauses:** “Calling this person either literate or illiterate” functions as subject. “They face” is a defining relative clause modifying “task”; the object relative pronoun “that” is omitted.

**Important grammar:** “Either … or …” presents alternatives. “Would” describes the consequence of an imagined classification. “Little” means not much, rather than “a little,” which emphasises some.

**Useful vocabulary:** literate; classification; the task someone faces.

**Why it sounds academic:** It challenges a category by asking how much explanatory value it provides.

**New IELTS-style example:** Labelling a city either successful or unsuccessful would tell us little about the problems its residents face.

### Sentence 2 — a question embedded inside a comparison

**Original, B:** “Educational attainment records completed levels of education, whereas reading ability concerns what someone can actually do with a text.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** Qualifications describe completed education; reading skill describes practical ability with written material.

**Structure:** Main clause + contrasting “whereas” clause, which contains an embedded what-clause.

**Main clause:** “Educational attainment records completed levels of education.”

**Dependent clauses:** “whereas reading ability concerns …” expresses contrast. “What someone can actually do with a text” functions as the complement of “concerns.”

**Important grammar:** The embedded clause uses statement order: “what someone can …,” not “what can someone …”. “Completed” modifies “levels”; it is not the sentence's main verb.

**Useful vocabulary:** educational attainment; reading ability; actually.

**Why it sounds academic:** It separates a recorded credential from the ability that credential might be assumed to represent.

**New IELTS-style example:** A driving certificate records formal qualification, whereas safe driving concerns what a person can actually do in changing conditions.

### Sentence 3 — temporary support with a passive relative clause

**Original, E:** “A useful lesson can scaffold a difficult task by supplying temporary support that is gradually withdrawn.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** A lesson can help a learner through a difficult task, then reduce that help as it becomes less necessary.

**Structure:** Main clause + “by” followed by an -ing clause expressing means; a relative clause modifies “support.”

**Main clause:** “A useful lesson can scaffold a difficult task.”

**Dependent clauses:** “supplying temporary support …” is a non-finite clause after “by.” “That is gradually withdrawn” is a defining passive relative clause describing the support.

**Important grammar:** “By supplying” explains how. “Is withdrawn” focuses on removal of support without naming the person removing it. “Can” presents a possibility, not a universal guarantee.

**Useful vocabulary:** scaffold a task; temporary support; gradually withdraw.

**Why it sounds academic:** It gives both the mechanism and the defining feature of a teaching approach.

**New IELTS-style example:** A training programme can develop independence by providing guidance that is gradually reduced.

### Sentence 4 — avoiding an unjustified assumption

**Original, F:** “Providers could accommodate such constraints through flexible timetables, without assuming that flexibility removes the need for structure.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** Services could offer adaptable schedules while still keeping enough organisation to support learning.

**Structure:** Main clause with “could” + a means phrase + “without” followed by a non-finite clause and an embedded content clause.

**Main clause:** “Providers could accommodate such constraints through flexible timetables.”

**Dependent clauses:** “assuming that flexibility removes the need for structure” follows “without.” “That flexibility removes the need for structure” states the assumption being rejected.

**Important grammar:** The understood subject of “assuming” is “providers.” “Such constraints” refers to the scheduling pressures illustrated immediately beforehand.

**Useful vocabulary:** accommodate constraints; flexible timetables; the need for structure.

**Why it sounds academic:** It proposes a response while identifying an overgeneralisation that should not follow from it.

**New IELTS-style example:** Universities could expand online access without assuming that convenience removes the need for personal feedback.

### Sentence 5 — a noun clause as subject

**Original, J:** “Whether new initiatives achieve that depends on teaching, access and the conditions in which people practise.”

**Plain-English paraphrase:** New programmes may or may not produce the intended result; teaching, access and practice conditions will influence it.

**Structure:** A whether-clause functioning as subject + main verb + a prepositional complement containing a relative clause.

**Main clause:** “[Whether new initiatives achieve that] depends on teaching, access and the conditions in which people practise.”

**Dependent clauses:** “Whether new initiatives achieve that” is the subject clause. “In which people practise” is a defining relative clause modifying “conditions.”

**Important grammar:** The subject clause takes singular “depends.” “That” refers to being better able to tackle unfamiliar tasks, described in the preceding sentence. “In which” is the formal equivalent of “where” in this context.

**Useful vocabulary:** initiatives; depends on; conditions in which.

**Why it sounds academic:** It keeps an outcome open and identifies the factors on which it depends.

**New IELTS-style example:** Whether a new park improves wellbeing depends on its design and the ways in which residents use it.

## IELTS WRITING TASK 2

**Spend about 40 minutes on this task. Write at least 250 words.**

In many places, adults who need to improve their reading and writing skills do not take part in educational programmes.

What are the main reasons for this? What measures could encourage more adults to participate?

Give reasons for your answer and include relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience.

### WRITING TASK 2 PLANNING

**Question type:** Two-part question: causes and solutions.

**What the question is really asking:** Explain non-participation among adults who could benefit, then propose measures that address those barriers. It is not asking why children struggle to learn or whether all education should be online.

**Possible thesis:** Adults may avoid courses because of scheduling pressures, stigma and a perceived lack of practical relevance; participation could improve through adaptable provision, respectful outreach and learning connected to everyday goals.

**Main argument 1 — causes:** Work and care responsibilities can make fixed classes difficult to attend. Stigma may discourage people from admitting a need for help. Explain how each barrier affects the decision to enrol, rather than listing many causes without development.

**Main argument 2 — responses:** Flexible schedules and accessible venues address practical constraints. Confidential advice and respectful communication address stigma. Useful tasks can make the value of learning clearer, but the measures should be tested against learners' actual needs.

**Counterargument or qualification:** Online provision can increase flexibility but may exclude people lacking suitable connections or confidence with digital tools. A mixture of supported in-person and self-paced opportunities may fit different learners better than one universal format.

**Relevant examples:** A hypothetical shift worker can attend different weekly sessions; a community library can offer discreet enquiries and support with realistic notices or forms. Present these as illustrations, not documented success stories.

**Suggested paragraph structure:**

1. Introduction: paraphrase the issue and outline the main causes and responses.
2. Body paragraph 1: explain two causes, showing their effect on participation.
3. Body paragraph 2: match specific measures to those causes and qualify their limits.
4. Conclusion: summarise the matched response without introducing a new cause.

**Useful vocabulary:** stigma, affordability, flexible, accommodate, relevance, facilitator, engagement, provision, autonomy.

**Useful collocations:** accommodate different needs; flexible learning arrangements; reduce stigma; practical relevance; accessible educational provision; encourage learner engagement.

**Common mistakes to avoid:**

- Blaming non-participation entirely on laziness without considering constraints.
- Describing the benefits of literacy but not answering why adults do not enrol.
- Proposing a solution that does not address any identified cause.
- Assuming phone ownership guarantees usable access to an online course.
- Confusing enrolment, engagement and lasting learning.
- Inventing research figures to make an example sound authoritative.

### 5 BAND 7.5–8.5 LANGUAGE PATTERNS

These are reusable structures, not guarantees of a particular band. Use them only when they serve a clear argument.

1. **One reason why [group] may not [action] is that [specific constraint].** Follow with a mechanism or example, not another unrelated cause.
2. **For people whose [circumstances], [apparently simple action] may involve [hidden cost].** The relative clause identifies the group precisely.
3. **By [appropriate response], providers could [benefit] without [creating another difficulty].** Keep the means connected to the stated barrier.
4. **The effectiveness of [measure] is likely to depend on whether [necessary condition].** An embedded whether-clause expresses uncertainty without vague hedging.
5. **Unless [barrier] is addressed, [resource] may remain inaccessible even to those who [motivation or need].** Explain why availability alone is insufficient.

**Before writing:** Draw a two-column plan with each cause paired to one response. Write your own essay first; no complete model essay is supplied.
