## 1. Main Reading

### When a Founder Becomes a Governance Test

**[P1]** On 17 November, Nvidia presented a remarkable set of results: quarterly revenue had reached $115.8 billion, more than twice the level of a year earlier. Yet before investors could concentrate on the company's next generation of chips, its new chairman addressed a different question. Jensen Huang, the co-founder who had led Nvidia for more than three decades, would not return as chief executive. It was reporting exceptional growth in the wake of a leadership crisis. The contrast raised a question: how much of a successful company's strength belongs to its founder, and how much belongs to the institution he helped build?

**[P2]** Huang had been the public face of Nvidia since its founding in 1993. He turned a graphics-chip business into a leading supplier for the artificial-intelligence industry. Success also created concentrated influence. A leader who knows the technology, customers and staff intimately can make bold decisions quickly. But when one person becomes almost inseparable from a brand, succession planning may receive less attention than it deserves. The challenge is not merely to replace a famous individual. It is to preserve the institutional memory, relationships and judgement that allowed the business to succeed.

**[P3]** The immediate crisis began with reports about Huang's personal relationships. He acknowledged marital infidelity and apologised. Separate reporting alleged relationships involving people connected to Nvidia, including a businesswoman whom US intelligence officials suspected of links to Chinese intelligence. China rejected the espionage allegations. Those suspicions have not been established by a public finding, and Nvidia said at the start of its independent investigation that it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised. This is where readers must draw a distinction between confirmed conduct, reported allegations and evidence of an actual security breach. They are not interchangeable. [^a][^b]

**[P4]** Why would a private relationship become a matter of governance? A relationship within a company's chain of command can create a conflict of interest, particularly if decisions about pay, promotion or complaints depend on the people involved. Connections that raise security concerns can also make an executive's judgement come under scrutiny. Neither situation automatically establishes wrongdoing beyond the disclosed conduct. Yet a board has to ask whether its disclosure rules, reporting channels and internal controls are strong enough to identify risks without giving a powerful founder special treatment. When those mechanisms fail, reputational damage can spread well beyond the individual concerned.

**[P5]** Nvidia's board accepted Huang's resignation on 11 November and announced it the following day. He stepped down as chief executive and left the board. Colette Kress, the company's long-serving finance chief, took the helm as interim CEO, while director Harvey Jones became board chairman. The board began looking for a permanent successor. These were more than changes on a chart: customers wanted to know whether the product roadmap would stay on schedule, employees wanted direction, and investors wanted a credible contingency plan. What was at stake was business continuity as well as the company's public reputation. [^c]

**[P6]** Five days later, a revealing disagreement emerged. Huang said he would be honoured to return if directors decided that doing so served the company, while an online petition calling for his reinstatement drew more than 200,000 signatures. Yet Jones told investors on 17 November that the board did not intend to reappoint him. Even after that refusal, high-profile pressure intensified: Elon Musk likened Huang's ousting to kicking Michael Jordan off a team during a winning streak, and President Donald Trump publicly urged Nvidia to give the “brilliant” founder “a second chance”. The board's stance illustrated the basic purpose of independent oversight: directors must be able to hold even an exceptionally successful chief executive accountable. A board that merely follows a celebrated founder—or yields to political and industry celebrity—cannot serve as a counterweight to executive power. It must face competing pressures without treating popularity as a substitute for its own judgement. [^d][^e][^f]

**[P7]** On the same day, 17 November, Nvidia announced a permanent board committee for national security and export-control matters. Chaired by director Suzanne Nora Johnson, it would oversee the company's risk management and work with independent outside specialists. Nvidia's internal security and trade-compliance teams would report to the committee as well as to the interim CEO. The design matters: board-level oversight can provide a route for concerns to reach directors rather than stopping inside the executive hierarchy. It can also help the company comply with complex regulatory obligations. But a committee's existence is only a starting point; its value depends on the questions it asks and the responses it demands. [^g]

**[P8]** The wider issue is particularly difficult for a company selling advanced computing equipment. AI chips are commercially valuable, but some are subject to US export controls because governments also regard them as strategically sensitive. Nvidia therefore faces a trade-off: it has incentives to expand sales while also having legal duties to safeguard restricted technology. Trouble arises when commercial ambition and compliance are guided by misaligned incentives. Effective systems must verify customers, examine unusual transactions and allow staff to raise concerns. They must translate a public commitment into action, not simply repeat it in an annual report. The independent investigation and the new committee will be judged against that practical standard.

**[P9]** Meanwhile, Nvidia's financial results complicated any simple narrative of collapse. Although its third-quarter revenue—up 103% year on year—reflected business completed before the leadership crisis, the company also issued a strong sales forecast for the current quarter. Kress described operations as continuing normally, pointing to a leadership team and customer relationships built over many years. That outlook suggested substantial underlying demand for Nvidia's products, even with its founder absent. The share price had been volatile after the news of his departure, but a market reaction is not the same thing as a verdict on governance. Strong sales cannot rule out weaknesses in internal controls; equally, a leadership shock does not automatically erase a company's engineering capability. [^h]

**[P10]** Inside the company, the test was more personal. At an all-staff meeting on 18 November, Kress sought to restore confidence and keep employees focused on the future. Reports said she discussed bringing forward decisions on pay and equity awards. Such measures may help retain people during a period of uncertainty, but stability also requires clarity about who makes decisions and how staff can challenge them. A founder can inspire extraordinary loyalty. For the next leadership team, the task is to preserve that energy while building an organisation that can function without constant reference to one personality. [^i]

**[P11]** Founder-led companies often benefit from speed, commitment and an unusually clear vision. Those strengths can also produce a governance problem when directors hesitate to challenge the person who delivered the success. Greater transparency, effective succession planning and a genuine willingness to investigate uncomfortable questions are safeguards against that risk. They may slow some decisions, but they can make the organisation more resilient when a crisis comes. This is not a choice between admiring a founder and respecting institutions. It is the harder task of ensuring that admiration does not weaken the rules.

**[P12]** Nvidia now has several tests ahead: the credibility of its investigation, the independence of its board, the effectiveness of its export-control oversight and the quality of its next CEO appointment. None can be settled by one earnings call or one announcement. What matters is whether people outside the leadership team can see that standards apply consistently and that difficult questions receive meaningful answers. If Nvidia can demonstrate this, it may restore confidence in something larger than a quarterly forecast: the idea that a company can outgrow its dependence on the person who made it famous.

## 2. Vocabulary in Context

The goal is not to memorise 30 items equally. Learn the **six Suggested Active** expressions well enough to use them without a prompt. Recognise the next ten quickly when reading; let the final fourteen become familiar through repeated exposure.

**★ CORE VOCABULARY** marks ten especially reusable expressions, including all six active items.

### A. Suggested Active — 6 expressions

1. **★ come under scrutiny** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to be examined or questioned closely.  
   **Here:** Huang's judgement was being examined because of the reported relationships and security concerns.  
   **Text:** “make an executive's judgement come under scrutiny.”  
   **Pattern:** `come under scrutiny over/for something`; `face public scrutiny`.  
   **New example:** The university's admissions policy came under scrutiny after students complained about unfair criteria.

2. **★ step down** *(phrasal verb)*  
   **Definition:** to leave a position of authority, often voluntarily or under pressure.  
   **Here:** Huang resigned from his leadership role.  
   **Text:** “He stepped down as chief executive and left the board.”  
   **Pattern:** `step down as + position`; `step down from + role`.  
   **New example:** The museum director stepped down after ten years to make room for new leadership.  
   **Learner note:** `Step down as chair` is more natural than `step down the chair`.

3. **★ at stake** *(phrase)*  
   **Definition:** in danger of being lost, damaged or affected by the outcome.  
   **Here:** Nvidia risked losing continuity and public trust.  
   **Text:** “What was at stake was business continuity as well as the company's public reputation.”  
   **Pattern:** `something is at stake`; `with so much at stake`; `what is at stake`.  
   **New example:** With farmers' livelihoods at stake, the town could not treat the water shortage as a minor inconvenience.

4. **★ draw a distinction between** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to identify and explain an important difference between two or more things.  
   **Here:** The article separates established facts from allegations and proof of a breach.  
   **Text:** “draw a distinction between confirmed conduct, reported allegations and evidence of an actual security breach.”  
   **Pattern:** `draw a clear/sharp distinction between A and B`.  
   **New example:** Teachers should draw a distinction between a student making an honest mistake and deliberately cheating.

5. **★ hold ... accountable** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to require someone to explain their actions and accept responsibility for them.  
   **Here:** Directors must be able to apply rules even to an admired founder.  
   **Text:** “hold even an exceptionally successful chief executive accountable.”  
   **Pattern:** `hold somebody accountable for something`; `be held accountable`.  
   **New example:** Residents want to hold the council accountable for delays in repairing the bridge.  
   **Learner note:** The person goes between *hold* and *accountable*: `hold managers accountable`, not `hold accountable managers`.

6. **★ restore confidence** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to make people trust someone or something again after uncertainty or disappointment.  
   **Here:** Kress tried to reassure employees during the transition.  
   **Text:** “restore confidence and keep employees focused on the future.”  
   **Pattern:** `restore confidence in + institution/person/system`; `help restore confidence`.  
   **New example:** Clear refund rules helped the airline restore confidence among passengers after the cancellations.

### B. Suggested Recognition — 10 expressions

7. **★ independent investigation** *(noun phrase)*  
   **Definition:** a formal examination conducted without control by the people being examined.  
   **Here:** Nvidia's board arranged a review of the reported security concerns.  
   **Text:** “at the start of its independent investigation”  
   **Pattern:** `launch/conduct an independent investigation into something`.  
   **New example:** The hospital commissioned an independent investigation into repeated complaints about patient care.

8. **★ conflict of interest** *(noun phrase)*  
   **Definition:** a situation in which personal interests may interfere with professional duties.  
   **Here:** A personal relationship could compromise workplace decisions.  
   **Text:** “can create a conflict of interest”  
   **Pattern:** `declare/disclose/avoid a conflict of interest`.  
   **New example:** A judge must disclose a conflict of interest if a close relative is involved in a case.

9. **★ a counterweight to** *(noun phrase)*  
   **Definition:** something that balances or limits the power or influence of something else.  
   **Here:** An independent board should challenge excessive executive power.  
   **Text:** “serve as a counterweight to executive power.”  
   **Pattern:** `act/serve as a counterweight to something`.  
   **New example:** Local journalism can serve as a counterweight to officials who rarely face public questions.

10. **★ safeguard** *(verb)*  
    **Definition:** to protect something important from harm or loss.  
    **Here:** Nvidia has duties to protect technology subject to restrictions.  
    **Text:** “legal duties to safeguard restricted technology.”  
    **Pattern:** `safeguard privacy/rights/data/security`.  
    **New example:** The school introduced new procedures to safeguard children's personal information.

11. **succession planning** *(noun phrase)*  
    **Definition:** preparation for replacing people in key positions.  
    **Here:** Long dependence on a founder may leave a company underprepared for his departure.  
    **Text:** “succession planning may receive less attention than it deserves.”  
    **Pattern:** `effective succession planning`; `invest in succession planning`.  
    **New example:** The family business began succession planning well before its owner retired.

12. **in the wake of** *(prepositional phrase)*  
    **Definition:** following an event, especially one with significant consequences.  
    **Here:** The strong earnings came shortly after a leadership crisis.  
    **Text:** “in the wake of a leadership crisis.”  
    **Pattern:** `in the wake of a crisis/disaster/decision`.  
    **New example:** The city redesigned its emergency alerts in the wake of the floods.

13. **comply with** *(verb phrase)*  
    **Definition:** to act according to a rule, requirement or law.  
    **Here:** Nvidia must meet legal and regulatory requirements.  
    **Text:** “help the company comply with complex regulatory obligations.”  
    **Pattern:** `comply with regulations/standards/a request`.  
    **New example:** Restaurants must comply with hygiene standards even during busy holiday periods.

14. **face competing pressures** *(verb phrase)*  
    **Definition:** to deal with demands that pull decisions in different directions.  
    **Here:** Directors had to consider support for Huang alongside institutional duties.  
    **Text:** “must face competing pressures without treating popularity as a substitute”  
    **Pattern:** `face competing pressures from A and B`.  
    **New example:** School leaders face competing pressures from parents who want more homework and students who want less.

15. **transparency** *(noun)*  
    **Definition:** openness that makes actions and decisions understandable and open to examination.  
    **Here:** Clear decision-making helps prevent unchecked leadership power.  
    **Text:** “Greater transparency, effective succession planning”  
    **Pattern:** `increase/improve transparency`; `transparency in decision-making`.  
    **New example:** Publishing the selection criteria would improve transparency in scholarship awards.

16. **trade-off** *(noun)*  
    **Definition:** a compromise in which gaining one benefit involves accepting a cost or limitation.  
    **Here:** Expanding sales and protecting restricted technology create different demands.  
    **Text:** “Nvidia therefore faces a trade-off”  
    **Pattern:** `a trade-off between A and B`; `make/accept a trade-off`.  
    **New example:** Working four days a week may involve a trade-off between free time and salary.

## 4. Answers and Explanations

### A. Active Recall — Answer Key

1. **came under scrutiny** — The past tense matches *after the report became public*; the subject is *procedures*.
2. **step down** — The infinitive follows *decided to*. You can also say *step down as head coach*.
3. **at stake** — Fixed phrase meaning that jobs may be lost.
4. **draw a distinction between ... and ...** — Both concepts must be linked with *between* and *and*.
5. **held accountable for** — Passive form: `be held accountable for + decision/action`.
6. **restore confidence** — The natural pattern is `restore confidence in + organisation`.

### B. Contextual Production — Model Responses

**Task 1:** “The principal stepped down after the errors damaged parents' trust. The school could restore confidence by publishing a clear correction plan and inviting external review.”  
**Why it works:** *Step down* names a leadership change; *restore confidence* requires action that can rebuild trust.

**Task 2:** “The app came under scrutiny when users learned that their information had been shared without clear consent. Regulators should hold its operators accountable for any proven breaches of privacy rules.”  
**Why it works:** The first expression describes examination, not a verdict. The second links responsibility to evidence.

**Task 3:** “We should draw a distinction between a temporary reduction in library hours and long-term cuts to road maintenance. Public access and safety are both at stake, but the risks develop differently.”  
**Why it works:** The contrast names two options precisely; *at stake* identifies what could be lost.

## 5. Deep Sentence Analysis

### Sentence 1 — Contrast without exaggeration

**Original:** “It was reporting exceptional growth in the wake of a leadership crisis.” **[P1]**

**Simpler version:** The business was growing very quickly even though it had just experienced a leadership crisis.

**How it works:** *In the wake of* places one development after another and suggests a meaningful connection. The sentence creates tension by placing *exceptional growth* beside *leadership crisis* without claiming the crisis caused the growth.

**Parallel example:** “The city announced a major housing investment in the wake of widespread criticism of its planning policies.”

### Sentence 2 — Separating categories of evidence

**Original:** “This is where readers must draw a distinction between confirmed conduct, reported allegations and evidence of an actual security breach.” **[P3]**

**Simpler version:** At this point, we need to separate established behaviour, unproven claims and proof that security was compromised.

**How it works:** *This is where* introduces the precise point in an argument at which a distinction matters. Three parallel noun phrases create a useful hierarchy of evidence. The phrase *draw a distinction between* is especially valuable in analytical writing.

**Parallel example:** “In debates about screen time, we should draw a distinction between using a device for research, using it for entertainment and using it compulsively.”

### Sentence 3 — Moving from intention to implementation

**Original:** “They must translate a public commitment into action, not simply repeat it in an annual report.” **[P8]**

**Simpler version:** The company needs to do practical things to keep its promise, rather than only talk about it.

**How it works:** `translate A into B` expresses conversion from an abstract idea to a concrete result. *Not simply* adds a compact contrast: public statements alone are insufficient.

**Parallel example:** “The council must translate its promise to improve cycling safety into protected lanes, not simply publish another strategy.”

## 6. IELTS Extension

### Writing Task 2

**Question:** Some people believe that the founders of successful companies should retain considerable freedom to make decisions. Others argue that independent boards must be able to limit the power of even the most successful founders. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Short outline:**

- **Introduction:** Define the tension between entrepreneurial freedom and effective oversight.
- **View 1:** Founders may have specialist knowledge, long-term vision and the confidence to act quickly.
- **View 2:** Independent directors can reduce conflicts of interest, improve transparency and provide a counterweight to concentrated influence.
- **Your position:** A strong founder can lead effectively, but oversight, succession planning and clear standards should not depend on personal popularity.
- **Conclusion:** Emphasise that reliable institutions support, rather than replace, individual talent.

**Useful expressions:** *face competing pressures; a counterweight to; succession planning; hold leaders accountable; safeguard long-term interests; a trade-off between speed and oversight*.

### Speaking Part 3

**Question 1:** Why do people sometimes find it difficult to criticise a very successful leader?

**Useful angles:** Past achievements can create loyalty and concentrated influence. Employees may fear damaging relationships or challenging established assumptions.  
**Useful expressions:** *come under scrutiny; face competing pressures; a conflict of interest*.

**Question 2:** What can an organisation do to rebuild trust after a public controversy?

**Useful angles:** Explain what is known, commission a genuinely independent review, communicate the findings and make practical changes.  
**Useful expressions:** *restore confidence; transparency; hold ... accountable; translate commitments into action*.

## 7. Today You Learned

**Three ideas**

1. A company's strong financial results do not, by themselves, resolve questions about leadership and governance.
2. Responsible analysis separates established facts, allegations and evidence before drawing conclusions.
3. Good oversight matters most when directors must challenge someone whose past success has earned exceptional loyalty.

**Six active expressions to retain**  
`come under scrutiny` · `step down` · `at stake` · `draw a distinction between` · `hold ... accountable` · `restore confidence`

**Three collocations to reuse**  
`launch an independent investigation` · `face competing pressures` · `translate a commitment into action`

**One output challenge:** Write **80–100 words** about a school, charity, sports club or company facing a loss of trust. Use **two or three active expressions** naturally. Include one concrete measure that would address the problem, not just a promise to improve.
