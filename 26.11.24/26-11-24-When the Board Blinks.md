## 1. Main Reading

### When the Board Blinks

**[P1]** Less than two weeks after stepping down in disgrace, Jensen Huang walked back into Nvidia's headquarters to the sound of cheering employees. On 23 November, the chipmaker announced that its board had voted unanimously the previous day to reinstate its co-founder as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Colette Kress stepped down as interim CEO and remained chief financial officer, while Harvey Jones remained chairman. On 17 November, Jones had told investors that the board did not intend to rehire Huang. Such an abrupt reversal rarely happens in a vacuum. It raised a sharper question than the one posed by his exit: when market panic and political pressure converge, how durable is a corporate board's independence?

**[P2]** Financial markets delivered an immediate verdict. Before the announcement, prediction markets had priced the odds of Huang returning by the end of November at as little as 3%. In early US trading on Monday, Nvidia's share price surged by more than 33%, helping to lift the broader market out of a week-long slump: the tech-heavy Nasdaq rose 6.7%, while the S&P 500 gained 4.6%. For many investors, the calculation was straightforward. Whatever doubts surrounded Huang's personal conduct, his technical vision and personal relationships across the supply chain were seen as irreplaceable. In the eyes of Wall Street, regaining the founder outweighed the governance risks of bringing him back. 

**[P3]** Yet the market's relief told only half the story. The decisive push appeared to come not from shareholders alone, but from the White House. President Donald Trump openly took credit for negotiating Huang's return. Asked by CNBC whether the board would have acted without him, Trump said: “I believe my intervention was very important.” Trump stated that the federal government had no reason to believe Huang's reinstatement posed a national security risk, citing Attorney General Todd Blanche's confirmation that authorities possessed no evidence of unlawful conduct. Nvidia's own statement noted that the board had received input from the US government before reversing course. 

**[P4]** That presidential endorsement blurred a boundary that modern corporate governance tries to keep clear. In principle, a public company's directors owe their legal duty to the company and its shareholders, not to elected officials. When a head of state publicly lobbies a board to rehire an executive—and then claims personal credit for the outcome—the line between private enterprise and political patronage becomes harder to see. Supporters argued that Trump was simply acting pragmatically to protect a strategic American champion in the global artificial-intelligence race. Critics countered that a board seen as bowing to political persuasion risks losing credibility the next time a crisis strikes.

**[P5]** Nowhere was that scepticism sharper than in Congress. Far from settling the controversy, Huang's reinstatement sparked bipartisan anger on Capitol Hill. Republican Senator John Cornyn questioned the government's assurance on social media, asking: “No national security risk? Based on what?” Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren called the board's decision “reckless” and demanded to know which officials had spoken to Nvidia and what they had said. Their frustration reflected a wider institutional tension: executive-branch assurances do not automatically satisfy lawmakers whose constitutional role is to oversee trade and national security. 

**[P6]** Nor did Huang's return close the legal files. Al Jazeera reported that multiple federal inquiries into Nvidia, including investigations by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Department of Justice (DOJ), remained active. More immediately, Huang still faces formal scrutiny in Washington. He is scheduled to sit for a transcribed interview with three House committees on 1 December, followed two days later by a public Senate Banking Committee hearing focused on US export controls and the sale of advanced AI chips to China. Returning to the corner office may have calmed investors, but it has not shielded the company from regulatory accountability. 

**[P7]** Aware of the optics, Nvidia's board paired Huang's return with a new governance safeguard. The company announced that it would recruit a Chief Compliance Officer—a newly created executive role responsible for overseeing compliance across the business and reporting directly to the board's Nominating and Corporate Governance Committee. The structure mirrors the national-security oversight committee established on 17 November: both are designed to ensure that compliance warnings can bypass the chief executive and reach independent directors. In theory, such guardrails allow a company to retain a powerful founder while keeping his authority in check. 

**[P8]** Still, institutional design is only as credible as the willingness to enforce it. Skeptics point out that the board's credibility was bruised not because it lacked committees, but because it reversed its own stated position within a week under intense external pressure. If directors can be persuaded to overturn a firm decision once a share price slides and a president intervenes, employees and regulators may wonder how fiercely a new compliance officer will be backed if another conflict arises. Promises of reform carry less weight when they appear to be concessions attached to a retreat.

**[P9]** Inside Nvidia, the mood was far more forgiving. Video footage showed staff welcoming Huang back with applause, and he told employees he intended to be “a better CEO the second time around.” For engineers and managers who spent years building products under his leadership, continuity matters deeply. A sudden leadership vacuum can stall morale and distract teams from fierce competition. Yet a warm reception in the atrium does not resolve the deeper structural problem: a company that rallies so dramatically around one individual's return has also demonstrated just how dependent on him it remains. 

**[P10]** The episode exposes a recurring dilemma in modern capitalism. Markets reward results, speed and singular talent; governance frameworks exist to enforce standards, restraint and long-term resilience. When the two collide, shareholders often prefer a rapid rebound in valuation over a slow, painful demonstration of institutional principle. Nvidia's early 33% surge suggests that Wall Street was willing to forgive a bruised process in exchange for a familiar leader. Whether that bargain holds will depend on what happens next—not in a single day's trading, but under the harsh lights of a Senate hearing room and inside the boardroom when the next difficult test arrives.

## 2. Vocabulary in Context

The goal is not to memorise 30 items equally. Learn the **six Suggested Active** expressions well enough to use them without a prompt. Recognise the next ten quickly when reading; let the final fourteen become familiar through repeated exposure.

**★ CORE VOCABULARY** marks ten especially reusable expressions, including all six active items.

### A. Suggested Active — 6 expressions

1. **★ reverse course** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to change a decision, policy or direction completely, often back to an earlier position.  
   **Here:** Nvidia's board changed its earlier refusal and brought Huang back.  
   **Text:** “received input from the US government before reversing course.”  
   **Pattern:** `reverse course on something`; `be forced to reverse course`.  
   **New example:** The airline reversed course on its baggage fee after widespread complaints from passengers.

2. **★ take credit for** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to claim praise or responsibility for a successful or notable outcome.  
   **Here:** Trump publicly claimed that his pressure led to Huang's reinstatement.  
   **Text:** “President Donald Trump openly took credit for negotiating Huang's return”  
   **Pattern:** `take credit for + noun / -ing`; `claim/deserve credit for`.  
   **New example:** Several ministers tried to take credit for the drop in inflation, even though global energy prices had fallen.

3. **★ blur a boundary** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to make the dividing line between two distinct areas or roles less clear.  
   **Here:** Presidential lobbying made the separation between government and corporate governance less distinct.  
   **Text:** “That presidential endorsement blurred a boundary that modern corporate governance tries to keep clear.”  
   **Pattern:** `blur the boundary/line between A and B`.  
   **New example:** Working from home has blurred the boundary between professional duties and personal rest.

4. **★ outweigh** *(verb)*  
   **Definition:** to be greater or more important than something else.  
   **Here:** Investors felt the benefits of Huang's return mattered more than the governance risks.  
   **Text:** “regaining the founder outweighed the governance risks of bringing him back.”  
   **Pattern:** `A outweighs B`; `the benefits outweigh the costs/risks`.  
   **New example:** For many commuters, the convenience of a direct train outweighs the slightly higher ticket price.  
   **Learner note:** *Outweigh* is a single verb—do not add *than* after it (`outweigh the risks`, not `outweigh than the risks`).

5. **★ keep ... in check** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to control or limit something so that it does not become excessive or harmful.  
   **Here:** New compliance structures are meant to limit a powerful CEO's authority.  
   **Text:** “retain a powerful founder while keeping his authority in check.”  
   **Pattern:** `keep power/spending/emotions in check`.  
   **New example:** Clear budget rules help keep public spending in check during an election year.

6. **★ carry weight** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to be influential, persuasive or taken seriously.  
   **Here:** Reform promises seem less convincing if they look like a reaction to a setback.  
   **Text:** “Promises of reform carry less weight when they appear to be concessions attached to a retreat.”  
   **Pattern:** `carry weight with somebody`; `carry little/less/more weight`.  
   **New example:** Recommendations from an independent panel usually carry more weight with voters than party slogans.

### B. Suggested Recognition — 10 expressions

7. **★ abrupt reversal** *(noun phrase)*  
   **Definition:** a sudden and unexpected change to the opposite decision or position.  
   **Here:** The board's rapid shift from rejecting Huang to reinstating him.  
   **Text:** “Such an abrupt reversal rarely happens in a vacuum.”  
   **Pattern:** `an abrupt/sudden reversal of policy/fortune`.  
   **New example:** The committee's abrupt reversal left local residents confused about whether the park would stay open.

8. **★ bipartisan** *(adjective)*  
   **Definition:** involving or supported by two opposing political parties.  
   **Here:** Both Republicans and Democrats criticised the reinstatement.  
   **Text:** “Huang's reinstatement sparked bipartisan anger on Capitol Hill.”  
   **Pattern:** `bipartisan support/opposition/criticism/bill`.  
   **New example:** The cybersecurity law passed with rare bipartisan support in both chambers.

9. **★ shield ... from** *(verb phrase)*  
   **Definition:** to protect someone or something from harm, criticism or legal pressure.  
   **Here:** Returning as CEO did not protect Nvidia from government inquiries.  
   **Text:** “it has not shielded the company from regulatory accountability.”  
   **Pattern:** `shield somebody/something from criticism/scrutiny/risk`.  
   **New example:** A famous brand name cannot shield a manufacturer from product-safety recalls.

10. **★ guardrails** *(plural noun)*

    **Here:** Oversight committees and a compliance chief meant to restrain executive power.  
    **Text:** “In theory, such guardrails allow a company to retain a powerful founder”  
    **Pattern:** `put guardrails in place`; `institutional/regulatory guardrails`.

11. **in a vacuum** *(prepositional phrase)*

## 5. Deep Sentence Analysis

### Sentence 1 — Framing a sharp analytical question

**Original:** “It raised a sharper question than the one posed by his exit: when market panic and political pressure converge, how durable is a corporate board's independence?” **[P1]**

**Simpler version:** His return created an even harder question than his resignation: can a company's board stay truly independent when both the stock market and politicians put pressure on it?

**How it works:** The colon sets up a direct, high-impact question. Using *converge* compresses two separate forces (*market panic* and *political pressure*) into a single moment of crisis, while *durable* treats independence as a material tested under stress.

**Parallel example:** “The ruling raised a sharper question than the original dispute: when economic urgency and environmental rules converge, how durable is a government's climate pledge?”

### Sentence 2 — Contrasting theory with reality

**Original:** “Promises of reform carry less weight when they appear to be concessions attached to a retreat.” **[P8]**

**Simpler version:** Promises to improve rules are less convincing if they look like compromises made only because leaders gave in.

**How it works:** *Carry less weight* is an idiomatic, formal way to express reduced credibility. Pairing *concessions* with *retreat* creates a crisp, sceptical tone typical of *The Economist* or *The Financial Times*.

**Parallel example:** “Assurances of transparency carry less weight when they appear to be concessions forced by a public scandal.”

### Sentence 3 — Balanced parallel contrast

**Original:** “Markets reward results, speed and singular talent; governance frameworks exist to enforce standards, restraint and long-term resilience.” **[P10]**

**Simpler version:** Stock markets care about fast results and brilliant individuals, whereas governance rules are meant to maintain standards, self-control and long-term stability.

**How it works:** A semicolon links two tightly mirrored clauses, each ending with a rhythmic list of three nouns. This parallel structure is ideal for summarising a complex tension in IELTS Writing Task 2 conclusions.

**Parallel example:** “Social media platforms reward outrage, speed and viral attention; public journalism exists to uphold accuracy, context and civic trust.”

## 6. IELTS Extension

### Writing Task 2

**Question:** Governments should never intervene in the leadership decisions of private companies, even when those companies are strategically important to the national economy. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Short outline:**

- **Introduction:** Acknowledge the strategic importance of major industries (such as AI, energy or banking), and state a clear thesis (e.g., governments should regulate industries through transparent laws, not lobby boards on individual personnel).
- **Body 1 (Why intervention is tempting):** In moments of crisis, officials may argue they are acting pragmatically to protect jobs, market stability or national competitiveness.
- **Body 2 (Why it is harmful):** Political interference blurs the boundary between private enterprise and political patronage, weakens board accountability and undermines investor trust in fair rules.
- **Conclusion:** Reiterate that strong legal guardrails—not personal political intervention—are the best way to protect national interests.

**Useful expressions:** *blur a boundary; political patronage; act pragmatically; keep power in check; institutional guardrails; carry weight*.

### Speaking Part 3

**Question 1:** Why do investors sometimes react positively even when a company's decision is criticised by regulators or politicians?

**Useful angles:** Investors often focus on short-term valuation, product continuity and proven leadership rather than long-term governance debates.  
**Useful expressions:** *outweigh the risks; rapid rebound; market slump; leadership vacuum*.

**Question 2:** When an organisation reverses a major decision quickly, how does that affect public trust?

**Useful angles:** It can look pragmatic if new evidence emerges, but if it appears to bow to pressure, future commitments may carry less weight.  
**Useful expressions:** *abrupt reversal; reverse course; bow to pressure; bruised credibility*.

## 7. Today You Learned

**Three ideas**

1. A corporate board's independence is tested most severely when market panic and political pressure converge at the same time.
2. A dramatic stock-price rebound reflects investor relief, not the resolution of legal, regulatory or congressional scrutiny.
3. New governance safeguards carry less credibility when they are introduced alongside a sudden retreat from an earlier principle.

`reverse course` · `take credit for` · `blur a boundary` · `outweigh` · `keep ... in check` · `carry weight`
